\title{Trajectory-exact constrained decoding for masked diffusion models,\\for any tractable constraint}
\author{Working notes}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Masked diffusion language models fill in a sequence over several steps, revising their predictions as positions
are revealed. Constrained decoders that enforce a constraint one step at a time ignore those revisions and sample
from the wrong distribution. TWISTER fixes this for constraints given by deterministic finite automata. These
notes separate the fix from the automaton: any structure that can compute the probability of a constraint under a
product distribution, and sample from that distribution restricted to the constraint, can take the automaton's
place: grammar charts, factor graphs and determinants, with ambiguous grammars
allowed. We give the
general theorem, the practical failure mode that shows up when the method meets a real model, three remedies with
guarantees, experiments on a text diffusion model and a synthetic testbed, and the state of an application to
mRNA sequence design.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%======================================================================

\subsection{The problem}
A masked diffusion language model \cite{sahoo2024} builds a sequence of length $L$ from a row of mask symbols over
$T$ steps. At each step a \emph{denoiser} reads the current partial sequence and predicts a distribution over the
vocabulary at every masked position; a \emph{scheduler} picks which positions to reveal; those positions are
filled independently from the predictions. We call this the \emph{native sampler} and write $p$ for the
distribution it induces. Within a step, the predictions are a product distribution across positions. Across
steps they change, because each denoiser call conditions on the positions revealed so far.

Fix a set $C$ of acceptable outputs. We want to sample from $p(x\mid x\in C)$. Rejection sampling is exact but
impractical when $p(C)$ is small, and cheaper methods distort the distribution. Token masking forbids tokens that
make $C$ unreachable, which ignores how much probability remains inside $C$ after each choice. \emph{Step-exact
decoding} \cite{de26} restricts the current product distribution to $C$ and samples from the result. It is also
biased, as the following example shows.

\begin{example}[Step-exact decoding is biased]\label{ex:bias}
Let $L=2$, $T=2$, reveal position 1 then position 2, and let $C=\{(a,a)\}$: the two tokens must agree. Write
$p^1(a)$ for the prediction at position 1 from the all-mask state, $p^2(a\mid\varnothing)$ for the prediction at
position 2 from that same state, and $p^2(a\mid a)$ for the prediction at position 2 after position 1 was revealed
as $a$. The native conditional is
\[
  p(x=(a,a)\mid C)\ \propto\ p^1(a)\,p^2(a\mid a).
\]
Step-exact decoding draws position 1 from the restricted product, giving $a$ with probability
$\propto p^1(a)\,p^2(a\mid\varnothing)$, and the second step is then forced. The two laws agree only when
$p^2(a\mid a)/p^2(a\mid\varnothing)$ is constant in $a$, that is, when revealing position 1 does not change the
model's prediction at position 2.
\end{example}

The ratio $p^2(a\mid a)/p^2(a\mid\varnothing)$ compares the constraint's probability under the updated predictions
with its probability under the earlier ones, and it is the importance weight that removes the bias. TWISTER
\cite{twister} applies this weight at every step to a population of particles and proves that the result targets
the native conditional exactly when $C$ is given by a deterministic finite automaton.

\subsection{Main result}

\begin{claimbox}{Main claim.}
TWISTER's correction does not require the constraint to be an automaton. It requires a structure that
(i) computes the constraint's probability under a product distribution, (ii) samples from that product
distribution restricted to the constraint, and (iii) tests membership exactly at the end. We call such a
structure a \emph{route}. Automata, grammar charts, factor graphs and determinants are all routes, and each
covers constraints the others cannot handle in polynomial time. The route may be ambiguous: a grammar that counts
a string once per parse tree still gives the correct target.
\end{claimbox}

The method has two parts. A route handles the computation within one step. A \emph{trajectory layer}, twisted
sequential Monte Carlo, handles the dependence across steps and uses the route only through its three
operations. \Cref{thm:traj} shows that this combination is exact.

\subsection{Further results}
Beyond \cref{thm:traj}, these notes contain three results.

\textbf{When the correction is unnecessary.} If the denoiser's predictions do not change as positions are
revealed, the frozen twist equals the ideal twist and step-exact decoding is already trajectory-exact
(\cref{prop:mf}). The size of the correction on a given model therefore depends on how much its predictions change
between steps, a quantity we call re-conditioning strength. It can be measured before running the sampler.

\textbf{Hidden mass under resampling.} The particle estimate $\Zh$ of $p(C)$ is unbiased for every number of
particles. When the current predictions greatly understate the probability of satisfying the constraint at an
intermediate step, adaptive resampling discards particles that would later have recovered, and part of $\E[\Zh]$
is carried by events too rare to observe in practice. On a text diffusion model the average of $\Zh$ fell 7\%
short of $p(C)$. \Cref{sec:practice} characterises this \emph{hidden mass} and gives three remedies that keep
$\Zh$ unbiased.

\textbf{Ambiguous routes and resampling.} With an ambiguous grammar, sampling without resampling has low error and
resampling increases it. Removing the effect exactly would require counting strings rather than derivations,
which is \#P-hard in general (\cref{sec:ambiguity}).

Each result is tagged \status{background} (known, cited), \status{ours} (proved here) or \status{proposed} (a
direction). \Cref{sec:summary} lists them all. Proofs longer than a few lines are in \cref{app:proofs}.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{Tokens and classes.}
Let $V$ be the vocabulary and $\mask\notin V$ the mask. Constraints are stated over a small alphabet $\Sigma$ of
token \emph{classes} through a map $\phi:V\to\Sigma$ (digit, opening bracket, codon). We write $C\subseteq\Sigma^L$
and say a token sequence $x$ satisfies $C$ if $\phi(x)\in C$. For a partial sequence $x\in(V\cup\{\mask\})^L$,
$\M(x)$ is its set of masked positions.

\paragraph{Native kernel.}
One native step from $x$ to $x'$ has probability
\begin{equation}\label{eq:native}
  \kappa_{t+1}(x'\mid x)=s_{t+1}(r\mid x)\prod_{i\in r}p^i_\theta(x'_i\mid x),
\end{equation}
where $r\subseteq\M(x)$ is the revealed set, $s_{t+1}$ the scheduler, and $p^i_\theta$ the denoiser's prediction.
The native path law is $p(x_{0:T})=\prod_t\kappa_{t+1}$, starting from $x_0=\mask^L$.

\paragraph{Reveal schedules.}
The scheduler is part of the process, not an implementation detail, and two choices are in common use. The
\emph{fixed} schedule reveals exactly $L/T$ positions per step, uniformly among the masked ones; this is
TWISTER's. The \emph{independent} schedule reveals each masked position with probability $1/(T-t)$, which is the
MDLM reverse step \cite{sahoo2024} under the log-linear noise schedule and leaves a random number of reveals per
step. Everything below holds for either, since $s_{t+1}$ cancels in \eqref{eq:potential}. But it must be the
\emph{same} scheduler in the proposal and in the native law being conditioned. The two laws are genuinely
different: on identical models and constraints, $p(C)$ differs between them by 4 to 10 standard errors at
$n=3\times10^5$ samples. mRNAutilus uses the independent schedule, so our mRNA runs do too.

\paragraph{Target.}
The \emph{Doob path law} $p^\star(x_{0:T})\propto p(x_{0:T})\one_C(x_T)$ has terminal marginal $p(\cdot\mid C)$,
and is a Markov chain with kernel $\kappa_{t+1}h_{t+1}/h_t$, where
\[
  h_t(x)=\Pr\nolimits_p(X_T\in C\mid X_t=x)
\]
is the \emph{Doob function}. It depends on the denoiser's outputs at every future state and cannot be computed at
feasible cost; the methods below replace it with computable twists. We write $h_0=p(C)$.

\paragraph{Frozen weights and the clamped sum.}
For a partial sequence $x$ the class weights are $w_i(\sigma\mid x)=\sum_{v:\phi(v)=\sigma}p^i_\theta(v\mid x)$ at
masked positions, and $w_i(\sigma\mid x)=[\sigma=\phi(x_i)]$ at revealed ones. The \emph{clamped sum} of a partial
sequence $x'$ under the predictions made at $x$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:clamped}
  Z(x';x)=\sum_{y\in C}\ \prod_{i\in\M(x')}w_i(y_i\mid x)\prod_{i\notin\M(x')}[y_i=\phi(x'_i)].
\end{equation}
Then $\eta_t(x):=Z(x;x)$ is the probability that $C$ holds if every masked position is filled independently from
the current predictions. TWISTER uses $\eta_t$ in place of $h_t$; we call it the \emph{frozen twist}, since it
treats the current predictions as final.

\paragraph{Twisted SMC.}
This material is standard \cite{delmoral2004}. A \emph{twist} is a sequence $\psi_0,\dots,\psi_T$ with
$\psi_T=\one_C$, defining intermediate targets $\gamma_t=p(x_{0:t})\psi_t(x_t)$ and \emph{potentials}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:G}
  G_{t+1}(x_t,x_{t+1})=\frac{\kappa_{t+1}(x_{t+1}\mid x_t)\,\psi_{t+1}(x_{t+1})}
                           {M_{t+1}(x_{t+1}\mid x_t)\,\psi_t(x_t)}
\end{equation}
for a proposal $M$. The product $\psi_0\prod_tG_{t+1}$ telescopes to $p\one_C/q$, so the \emph{final} weight does
not depend on the twist; the twist changes only the intermediate weights, and hence which particles survive
resampling. The ideal twist is $h_t$. With $K$ particles, resampling fires when the effective sample size
$\mathrm{ESS}=(\sum_kW^k)^2/\sum_k(W^k)^2$ drops below a threshold, and
$\Zh=\prod_{\text{resamplings}}\big(\tfrac1K\sum_kW^k\big)\times\big(\tfrac1K\sum_kW^k\big)_{\text{final}}$
is unbiased for $h_0$ at every $K$.

\section{Routes}\label{sec:routes}
%======================================================================

\begin{definition}[Route]\label{def:route}
A \emph{route} for $C$ provides three operations on weights $w\ge0$:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $\op{Mass}(w)=\sum_yd(y)\prod_iw_{i,y_i}$, for a fixed $d\ge0$ with $\operatorname{supp}d\supseteq C$;
  \item $\op{Sample}(w)$, an exact draw from $y\propto d(y)\prod_iw_{i,y_i}$;
  \item $\op{Accepts}(y)=\one_C(y)$, an exact membership test.
\end{itemize}
It is \emph{exact} if $d=\one_C$, \emph{derivation-weighted} if $d\in\mathbb N$ with $\operatorname{supp}d=C$, and
a \emph{superset route} if $\operatorname{supp}d\supsetneq C$.
\end{definition}

The trajectory layer requires that $\op{Sample}$ draw from exactly the measure whose total $\op{Mass}$ returns.
The function $d(y)$ counts \emph{derivations}: accepting paths of an automaton, parse trees of a grammar. A route
is unambiguous when $d\equiv1$ on $C$.

\Cref{tab:routes} lists the four families; \cref{app:routes} gives the recursions, the samplers and their references.

\end{table}

Which route is cheapest depends on the representation as well as the constraint. The same-order copy relation
$y_i=y_{i+k}$ is width-1 as a factor graph and needs $|\Sigma|^k$ states as an in-order automaton. A compiler
estimates the cost of each candidate from compile-time statistics and picks the cheapest. When none fits the
budget, or the family has \#P-hard counting, it refuses the constraint; it does not approximate.

\subsection{Ambiguity depends on the route}
The same language can have an unambiguous route and an ambiguous one. Unambiguous Dyck-1 is
$S\to(S)S\mid\varepsilon$; ambiguous Dyck-1 is $S\to SS\mid(S)\mid()$, which generates the same strings but gives
a string with several adjacent blocks several parse trees. Whether ambiguity matters depends on the semiring in
which the route is evaluated.

\begin{proposition}[\status{background}]\label{prop:semiring}
Let a structure circuit $S$ represent $C$. Evaluated on leaves $w\ge0$, the Boolean semiring answers
completability and max-times answers MAP, both regardless of ambiguity; sum-product returns
$\sum_yd_S(y)\prod_iw_{i,y_i}$, which is the constrained probability \emph{if and only if} $d_S\equiv1$ on $C$.
\end{proposition}
\proofref{A special case of Wang et al.\ \cite{wang2024atlas}, Thm.~4 and Ex.~2: mapping a Boolean circuit into a
semiring is tractable when the semiring is idempotent or the circuit is deterministic. The underlying fact is
older and more general: the correctness of a sum-product recursion uses only distributivity and the
commutation of nested sums, so any commutative semiring gives a valid algorithm. This is the generalized
distributive law of Aji and McEliece \cite{aji2000}.}

Idempotent addition counts the $d_S(y)$ copies of a string once; ordinary addition counts all of them. An
ambiguous route therefore samples from the derivation-weighted law $\propto d(y)\prod_iw_{i,y_i}$, which is why
Dang and Ermon call their NFA variant a ``soft proxy'' \cite{de26}. One corollary: Viterbi on the length-$L$ chart
computes exact constrained MAP for \emph{every} context-free grammar, inherently ambiguous ones included, in
$O(L^3|G|)$. \Cref{thm:traj} shows that trajectory-level sampling is also exact for ambiguous routes, although
single-step sampling is not.

\subsection{The tractability boundary}
Step-exact decoding needs the constrained probability and its marginals. Both are queries on the
\emph{constraint polynomial} $P_C(w)=\sum_{y\in C}\prod_iw_{i,y_i}$, and the following theorem characterises when
they are tractable.

\begin{theorem}[\status{background}]\label{thm:char}
Fix $\Sigma$ and a family $\{C_L\}$, with all bounds polynomial in $L$ and $|\Sigma|$. These are equivalent:
(a) $P_{C_L}$ has a polynomial-size arithmetic circuit over $\mathbb R$, where subtraction is allowed and the circuit
need not be decomposable, deterministic or monotone; (b) a real-RAM algorithm computes $P_{C_L}(w)$ for every
$w\ge0$; (c) a real-RAM algorithm computes all constrained marginals whenever $P_{C_L}(w)>0$. Given a circuit of
size $s$, $P_C$ costs $O(s)$, all $L|\Sigma|$ marginals together cost $O(s)$, and an exact sample costs $O(Ls)$.
\end{theorem}
\proofref{(b)$\Rightarrow$(a) is Broadrick et al.\ \cite{broadrick2025}, Prop.~5.4. Their virtual-evidence
marginalisation is query (b) for $\one_C$ after one-hot encoding; we checked that their division-elimination and
polynomial-identity steps go through for $P_C$ with correctness only on the positive orthant. (a)$\Rightarrow$(b)
is evaluation; marginals come from one reverse-mode pass. (c)$\Rightarrow$(b) and the sampler are
self-reducibility under clamping \cite{jvv86}.}

The theorem requires tractability for arbitrary weights $w\ge0$. Tractable marginals under hard evidence alone do
not imply a small circuit \cite{broadrick2025}, and decoding needs arbitrary weights because the weights are the
model's predictions. Decomposable circuits, which include automata, charts and bounded-width factor graphs, do not
cover every tractable family:

\begin{remark}[Spanning trees separate the two \status{background}]\label{rem:arb}
Encode a tree on $\{0,\dots,n\}$ by head pointers and let $C$ be the arborescences rooted at $0$. Then $P_C$ is
the directed spanning-tree polynomial: polynomial-size via the matrix-tree theorem, but every \emph{monotone}
circuit for it has size $2^{\Omega(n)}$ \cite{jerrumsnir1982}. Smooth decomposable circuits with non-negative
parameters are monotone, so no automaton, grammar or bounded-treewidth route is polynomial here, while exact
decoding through a determinant is. The determinant route is included for this case.
\end{remark}

If a family has no FPRAS, it has no polynomial-time almost-uniform sampler either \cite{jvv86}, so the compiler's
refusal of such a family reflects a limit of the problem, not of the implementation.

\section{The route-generic algorithm}\label{sec:traj}
%======================================================================

Write $Z_d(x';x)$ for the clamped sum \eqref{eq:clamped} with $\one_C$ replaced by the route's $d$, and
$\eta_t(x)=Z_d(x;x)$.

\begin{lemma}[Step-exact proposal \status{background}]\label{lem:prop}
The kernel
$M_{t+1}(x'\mid x)=s_{t+1}(r\mid x)\prod_{i\in r}p^i_\theta(x'_i\mid x)\,Z_d(x';x)/Z_d(x;x)$
is a probability kernel, sampled by drawing $r$ from the native scheduler, drawing a complete class sequence
$y\sim\op{Sample}(w(x))$ with revealed positions clamped, drawing tokens within classes at the positions in $r$,
and revealing only those.
\end{lemma}
\proofref{TWISTER \cite{twister}, Prop.~3.6, stated for DFAs. The proof uses only that the sampler draws from the
measure whose total is $Z_d(x;x)$, so it carries over to any route.}

Combining \cref{lem:prop} with \eqref{eq:G}, the scheduler and the token probabilities cancel:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:potential}
  G_{t+1}(x,x')=\frac{Z_d(x;x)}{\psi_t(x)}\cdot\frac{\psi_{t+1}(x')}{Z_d(x';x)}.
\end{equation}
With TWISTER's choice $\psi_t=\eta_t$ this is $G_{t+1}=Z_d(x';x')/Z_d(x';x)$, the mass of the new partial sequence
under the new predictions divided by its mass under the old ones. In \cref{ex:bias} it equals
$p^2(a\mid a)/p^2(a\mid\varnothing)$.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Route-generic twisted SMC (one run with $K$ particles)}\label{alg:smc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require route $(\op{Mass},\op{Sample},\op{Accepts})$; denoiser; $T$; $K$; ESS threshold $\tau$;
  $\epsilon\in[0,1)$; $\beta\in(0,1]$
\State for each $k$: $x^k\gets\mask^L$, $\eta^k\gets\op{Mass}(w(x^k))$, $\log W^k\gets\beta\log\eta^k$;
  $\log\Zh\gets0$
\For{$t=0,\dots,T-1$}
  \For{each particle $k$}
    \State draw $r\sim s_{t+1}(\cdot\mid x^k)$ and $y\sim\op{Sample}(w(x^k))$; draw tokens within classes on $r$;
      $x'\gets x^k$ revealed on $r$
    \State $z\gets Z_d(x';x^k)$ \Comment{mass of $x'$ under the old predictions}
    \If{$t<T-1$} call the denoiser at $x'$; $\eta'\gets Z_d(x';x')$; $\psi'\gets(\eta')^\beta$
    \Else\ $\psi'\gets\op{Accepts}(x')$ \Comment{exact membership at the last step}
    \EndIf
    \State $\log W^k\gets\log W^k+(1-\beta)\log\eta^k+\log\psi'-\log z$; \ $x^k\gets x'$; \ $\eta^k\gets\eta'$
  \EndFor
  \If{$t<T-1$ and $\mathrm{ESS}(W)<\tau K$}
    \State $\log\Zh\gets\log\Zh+\log\frac1K\sum_kW^k$; \ $\wt\gets W/\sum_kW^k$; \
      $\pi\gets(1-\epsilon)\wt+\epsilon/K$
    \State draw ancestors $a_1,\dots,a_K$ from $\pi$; for each $k$: $x^k\gets x^{a_k}$, $\eta^k\gets\eta^{a_k}$,
      $W^k\gets\wt_{a_k}/\pi_{a_k}$
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State $\log\Zh\gets\log\Zh+\log\frac1K\sum_kW^k$
\State \Return weighted particles $\{(x^k,W^k)\}$ and $\Zh$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\Cref{alg:smc} replaces TWISTER's automaton with a route and adds two options from \cref{sec:practice}: defensive
mixing $\epsilon$ and twist power $\beta$. With an automaton route, $\epsilon=0$ and $\beta=1$, it is TWISTER. At
$K=1$ with the weights ignored it is step-exact decoding \cite{de26}. Per particle and step it costs one denoiser
call, one route sample and two mass evaluations, the second of which is reused at the next step.

\begin{theorem}[Route-generic trajectory exactness \status{ours}]\label{thm:traj}
Assume the denoiser gives every token positive probability, the route satisfies $\operatorname{supp}d\supseteq C$
with ambiguity allowed, and the twists satisfy $\psi_t>0$ wherever $h_t>0$ with $\psi_T=\one_C$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),nosep]
  \item $q(x_{0:T})\,W(x_{0:T})=p(x_{0:T})\,\one_C(x_T)$ for every path;
  \item the weighted particles target the Doob path law and are consistent for $p(\cdot\mid C)$ as $K\to\infty$;
  \item $\E[\Zh]=h_0$ for every $K\ge1$, every $\epsilon\in[0,1)$, and every resampling rule whose decision
    depends only on the current particles and weights;
  \item the derivation count affects only the variance, since the factor $d(x_T)$ from the last proposal step
    cancels in the last potential.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\noindent\textit{Proof idea.} By \cref{lem:prop} the route's clamped sums appear in both the proposal density and
the potential and cancel, leaving a product of twists that telescopes to $p\,\psi_T$. Part (iv) explains why
ambiguity does not change the target: at the last step $x_T$ is fully revealed, so $Z_d(x_T;x_{T-1})=d(x_T)$ appears in the
denominator of the final potential and cancels the $d(x_T)$ that the proposal introduced. Full proof in
\cref{app:proofs}. \smallskip

The two kinds of decoding therefore have different requirements. Step-exact decoding needs an exact route
(\cref{thm:char}). Trajectory-exact decoding needs a route whose sampler matches its mass, a twist that is
positive on completable states, and an exact membership test at the last step; any NFA or CFG chart meets these
in polynomial time per particle. This is consistent with the \#P-hardness of computing $h_t$ for general CFGs,
because SMC never computes $h_t$ and its samples are exact only as $K\to\infty$.

\begin{proposition}[Terminal-twist pitfall \status{ours}]\label{prop:terminal}
If an ambiguous route's own value $Z_d(x_T;x_T)=d(x_T)$ is used as the terminal twist in place of $\one_C$, the
particles target $\propto p(x_{0:T})\,d(x_T)$, the derivation-weighted law rather than the one asked for.
\end{proposition}
\proofref{Replace $\psi_T=\one_C$ by $\psi_T=d$ in the telescoping product of \cref{thm:traj}(i).}

At a fully revealed state $\op{Mass}$ returns $d(x_T)$, which equals $\one_C(x_T)$ only for an exact route. The
last step must therefore call $\op{Accepts}$.

\begin{remark}[Relation to prior work]
For unambiguous routes \cref{thm:traj} is TWISTER's argument \cite{twister} (Thm.~4.2) applied to a different
route; their proof uses the DFA only to compute $Z$. Dang et al.\ \cite{dang2026icml} observe, for NFAs in
autoregressive SMC, that the derivation-weighted distribution is still a valid proposal. \Cref{thm:traj} combines
these for masked diffusion and every route of \cref{sec:routes}. We have not found the terminal-twist pitfall or
the analysis of \cref{sec:practice} stated elsewhere.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Choice of twist}
The proof of \cref{thm:traj} uses only two facts: the proposal density is computable, and the twists telescope.
The twist can therefore be chosen independently of the proposal. The remedies in \cref{sec:practice} use this.

\begin{proposition}[Free intermediate twists \status{background}]\label{prop:free}
Let $M_{t+1}$ be any kernel with computable density, positive wherever $\kappa_{t+1}h_{t+1}>0$, and let
$\psi_t\ge0$ be positive where $h_t>0$ with $\psi_T=\one_C$. Then $qW=p\one_C$, and (ii) and (iii) of
\cref{thm:traj} hold. The same is true for \emph{random} twists $\psi_t(x,\xi_t)$, provided the realisation
$\xi_t$ is reused in the numerator at step $t$ and the denominator at step $t+1$.
\end{proposition}
\proofref{The deterministic case is the standard twisted-SMC construction \cite{delmoral2004}; \cite{zhao2024}
uses it for autoregressive language models. For random twists, include $\xi_t$ in the state; each realisation
appears once in a numerator and once in a denominator, so the product still telescopes path by path.}

\subsection{A diagnostic identity}
\begin{lemma}[\status{ours}]\label{lem:twosided}
Under the conditions of \cref{thm:traj},
$\E_q[W]=h_0$ and $\E_p[\one_C(x_T)/W]=\Pr_q(x_T\in C)$, the second expectation taken over native paths. For an
exact or derivation-weighted route the right-hand side is 1.
\end{lemma}
\proofref{By \cref{thm:traj}(i), $\E_p[\one_C/W]=\sum_{\text{paths}}p\,\one_C\,q/(p\,\one_C)$, the
$q$-probability of ending in $C$.}

The first identity can be checked with proposal runs and the second with native samples, and the two checks
detect different errors. Native paths whose weight exceeds every observed proposal weight indicate regions the
proposal rarely visits. Running \cref{alg:smc} with resampling off alongside the main run separates
proposal-and-twist error from resampling error; the problem described in \cref{sec:practice} was found this way.

%======================================================================
\section{When the correction is unnecessary}\label{sec:meanfield}
%======================================================================

With $\psi_t=\eta_t$ the potential \eqref{eq:potential} is $G_{t+1}=Z_d(x';x')/Z_d(x';x)$, which differs from 1
only because the denoiser's predictions change between steps. This section treats the case where they do not.

\begin{definition}[Trajectory mean-field \status{ours}]\label{def:mf}
A denoiser is \emph{trajectory mean-field} if $p^i_\theta(\cdot\mid x)=p^i_\theta(\cdot\mid\tilde x)$ for every $i$
and every pair of partial sequences with $\M(x)=\M(\tilde x)$: the prediction at a masked position depends on
which positions are masked, but not on the values revealed at the others.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Mean-field collapse \status{ours}]\label{prop:mf}
Let the denoiser be trajectory mean-field, let $\psi_t=\eta_t$ for $t<T$ and $\psi_T=\one_C$, and take an exact
route. Then $\eta_t=h_t$ everywhere and $G_{t+1}=1$ almost surely, so with $K=1$ and no resampling,
$\Zh=\eta_0=h_0$ deterministically and the single trajectory is an exact draw from $p(\cdot\mid C)$. Step-exact
decoding is already trajectory-exact.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $w_i(\cdot)$ for the common prediction at masked position $i$, which by \cref{def:mf} does not depend on the
revealed values. The remaining positions are then filled independently from $w$ whatever order the scheduler
chooses, so $h_t(x)=\sum_{y\in C}\prod_{i\in\M(x)}w_i(y_i)\prod_{i\notin\M(x)}[y_i=\phi(x_i)]$, which is
\eqref{eq:clamped} at $x'=x$, that is $\eta_t(x)$. For the potential, $Z(x';x)$ and $Z(x';x')$ clamp the same
positions and differ only in which denoiser call supplied the weights at the masked ones; those weights coincide,
so the sums are equal and $G_{t+1}=1$. At the last step $Z(x_T;x_{T-1})=\one_C(x_T)=\psi_T(x_T)$ and the ratio is
again 1. Constant potentials make $\Zh$ deterministic, and $M$ is then the Doob kernel.
\end{proof}

For a mean-field model one particle suffices. The constraint still couples the positions, so $p(\cdot\mid C)$ is
not a product distribution, but the route's sampler accounts for that coupling exactly in a single draw. The
particles and weights correct only for re-conditioning, the change in the denoiser's predictions as positions are
revealed. We define the \emph{re-conditioning strength} as the typical size of
\[
  \log G_{t+1}=\log Z(x';x')-\log Z(x';x),
\]
the change in the route's mass caused only by the model revising its predictions. It is zero for a mean-field
model.

On a model with small re-conditioning strength, step-exact decoding is already close to unbiased and the particle
correction changes little. Re-conditioning strength can be measured before building a sampler, by revealing
context and recording how the predicted distribution at a held-out masked position changes (\cref{sec:expmrna}).

\section{Hidden mass under resampling}\label{sec:practice}
%======================================================================

\Cref{thm:traj}(iii) gives $\E[\Zh]=h_0$. If much of this expectation comes from rare events, the average of $\Zh$
over any feasible number of runs falls short of $h_0$. On OWT-130M with TWISTER's experimental setup, plain
resampling gave $\Zh/h_0=0.930$ (CI $0.910$--$0.949$) over $10^5$ runs, while the same configuration without
resampling gave $0.998$.

\subsection{Continuation values and hidden mass}
\begin{lemma}[Continuation value \status{ours}]\label{lem:cont}
Without further resampling, the expected product of the remaining potentials from state $x$ at step $s$ is
$V_s(x)=\E_q[\prod_{t\ge s}G_{t+1}\mid x_s=x]=h_s(x)/\psi_s(x)$.
\end{lemma}
\proofref{From step $s$ on, the proposal kernels and potentials telescope to $p\,\psi_T/\psi_s$ along every path;
sum over continuations.}

Suppose resampling fires at step $s$. Writing $W^k_s=\rho^k_s\,\psi_s(x^k_s)$ with $\rho^k_s$ the path ratio
$p/q$ up to $s$, \cref{lem:cont} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mismatch}
  \wt_k\propto\rho^k_s\,\psi_s(x^k_s),\qquad
  c_k\propto\rho^k_s\,h_s(x^k_s),
\end{equation}
where $c_k$ is particle $k$'s share of $\E[\Zh\mid\mathcal F_s]$. Resampling allocates offspring in proportion to
$\psi_s$, while each particle's share of the estimate is proportional to $h_s$. Where the frozen twist understates
$h_s$, resampling is likely to remove particles that carry a large share of the estimate.

\begin{proposition}[Hidden mass under multinomial resampling \status{ours}]\label{prop:hidden}
Under multinomial resampling, particle $k$ has at least one offspring with probability
$1-(1-\wt_k)^K\le K\wt_k$, and when it does its contribution is multiplied by about $1/(K\wt_k)$. Define the
hidden mass $H_\varepsilon=h_0^{-1}\,\E[\sum_kc_k\one\{\wt_k<\varepsilon\}]$. Over $R$ runs, the expected number
of runs in which any particle with $\wt_k<\varepsilon$ leaves an offspring is at most
$RK\varepsilon\,\E[N_\varepsilon]$, where $N_\varepsilon$ is the number of such particles. When this is much less
than 1, the average of $\Zh$ over the $R$ runs misses
about a fraction $H_\varepsilon$ of $h_0$ with high probability, even though $\E[\Zh]=h_0$.
\end{proposition}
\proofref{Offspring counts are binomial$(K,\wt_k)$, giving the first bound; a union bound over particles and runs
gives the second. On the event that particle $k$ has $n_k$ offspring they carry weight $\bar W$ each, against an
expected share $c_k$ that \cref{lem:cont} assigns to weight $K\bar W\wt_k$; hence the factor $n_k/(K\wt_k)$.}

Two consequences follow. First, tail diagnostics fitted to observed values of $\Zh$, such as the
generalised-Pareto shape estimate, cannot detect the missing mass, because the events that carry it do not appear
in the sample. Second, if $\psi_s=h_s$ then $c_k=\wt_k\sum_jc_j$ by \eqref{eq:mismatch}, so
$H_\varepsilon\le K\varepsilon$. Large hidden mass therefore requires twist error of one kind: states at which the
frozen twist is much smaller than $h_s$, that is, over-confident that the constraint has become unreachable.

On OWT-130M, at the first step where resampling would fire, 13\% of particles had normalised weight below
$10^{-6}$ and together carried 4.7\% of $h_0$. Their median log twist was $-40$, and 19\% of them had a twist
value above $10^{-3}$ by the end of the run.

\begin{remark}[Estimating $H_\varepsilon$]
Runs with resampling on and off are identically distributed up to the first step resampling would fire. So
$H_\varepsilon$ is estimable from resampling-off runs: record every particle's weight at every step, find the
first step with $\mathrm{ESS}<\tau K$, and sum the final contributions of particles whose normalised weight there
was below $\varepsilon$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Synthetic testbeds]
$\eta_t$ evaluates the constraint under the current predictions, and nothing forces those to be consistent with
what the denoiser will predict later. A real model can assign a completion almost zero probability at one step
and substantial probability a few reveals on. A synthetic denoiser whose predictions depend weakly on context has
little hidden mass by construction, so tests on such a model are unlikely to show the problem.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Three remedies}
All three keep $\E[\Zh]=h_0$ and can be combined. Defensive resampling changes the ancestor distribution,
tempering changes the power of the twist, and calibration changes the law under which the twist is computed.

\paragraph{Defensive resampling.}
Resample ancestors from $\pi=(1-\epsilon)\wt+\epsilon/K$ instead of $\wt$, giving each offspring weight
$\wt_a/\pi_a$.

\begin{proposition}[\status{ours}]\label{prop:defensive}
With this rule: $\E[\Zh]=h_0$; every post-resampling weight is at most $1/(1-\epsilon)$ and every particle
survives with probability at least $1-e^{-\epsilon}$ \emph{however small its weight}; and for any non-negative
continuation values the resampling step adds a conditional relative variance of at most $1/\epsilon$. Under
multinomial resampling the corresponding quantity has no bound when a particle with small $\wt_k$ has a large
continuation value.
\end{proposition}
\proofref{In \cref{app:proofs}.}

The variance bound $1/\epsilon$ and the survival probability $1-e^{-\epsilon}$ hold however far the twist
understates $h_s$. The construction is defensive importance sampling \cite{hesterberg1995} applied to the ancestor
distribution. When the twist is accurate, the mixture makes resampling somewhat less efficient. On OWT-130M,
$\epsilon=0.25$ raised $\Zh/h_0$ from $0.930$ to $0.998$.

\paragraph{Tempered twists.}
Raise the frozen twist to a power $\beta\in(0,1]$.

\begin{proposition}[\status{ours}]\label{prop:tempered}
For $\beta\in(0,1]$ let $\psi_t=\eta_t^\beta$ for $t<T$ and $\psi_T=\one_C$, with the step-exact proposal. Then
the potential is $G_{t+1}=\eta_t(x)^{1-\beta}\eta_{t+1}(x')^\beta/Z_d(x';x)$, parts (ii) and (iii) of
\cref{thm:traj} hold, and in \eqref{eq:mismatch}
\[
  \frac{c_k}{\wt_k}\ \propto\ h_s(x^k_s)^{1-\beta}\Big(\frac{h_s(x^k_s)}{\eta_s(x^k_s)}\Big)^{\beta}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\proofref{In \cref{app:proofs}.}

An over-confidence factor $\delta^{-1}=h_s/\eta_s$ therefore enters the resampling mismatch as $\delta^{-\beta}$.
Smaller $\beta$ uses less of the twist's information: as $\beta\to0$ particles are resampled on the path ratio
alone, and $\beta=1$ is TWISTER.

\begin{remark}[When tempering is not enough]
Tempering removes hidden mass only if the tempered mismatch is large enough to be sampled. By
\cref{prop:hidden} a particle suppressed by $\delta^\beta$ leaves an offspring in one of $R$ runs only if
$RK\delta^\beta\gtrsim1$, requiring $\beta\lesssim\log(RK)/\log(1/\delta)$. When the twist is over-confident by
many orders of magnitude this forces $\beta$ near 0, which discards the twist's information. Defensive resampling
has no such condition, so we use it by default and add tempering when the over-confidence is moderate.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Calibrated twists.}
Calibration changes the law under which the twist is computed. The frozen twist fills masked positions from the
current class weights $w_i$; any other product law $u_i$ over the masked positions gives a twist that the route
evaluates exactly, at the same cost. We use $u_i\propto w_i^\tau$ with $\tau<1$, which flattens the weights.

\begin{proposition}[Calibrated twist \status{ours}]\label{prop:calibrated}
Let $\psi_t=\eta^u_t$ for $t<T$ and $\psi_T=\one_C$, and let $Z^{u,r}_d(x;x)$ be the clamped sum using $w$ on the
reveal set $r$ and $u$ elsewhere. Then
$M^u_{t+1}(x'\mid x)=s_{t+1}(r\mid x)\prod_{i\in r}p^i_\theta(x'_i\mid x)\,Z^u_d(x';x)/Z^{u,r}_d(x;x)$
is a probability kernel; it is the step-exact proposal for $\psi=\eta^u$; the potential is
$G_{t+1}=\big(Z^u_d(x';x')/Z^u_d(x;x)\big)\big(Z^{u,r}_d(x;x)/Z^u_d(x';x)\big)$; and (ii), (iii) of
\cref{thm:traj} hold. At $u=w$ everything reduces to \cref{lem:prop}.
\end{proposition}
\proofref{In \cref{app:proofs}.}

\begin{lemma}[Calibration floor \status{ours}]\label{lem:floor}
For $u_i\propto w_i^\tau$ with $\tau\in(0,1]$ and $m=|\M(x)|$,
$|\Sigma|^{-m(1-\tau)}\eta_t(x)^\tau\le\eta^u_t(x)$. Hence if the frozen twist is over-confident by a factor
$\delta$, the calibrated twist satisfies $h_t/\eta^u_t\le|\Sigma|^{m(1-\tau)}\delta^{-\tau}$.
\end{lemma}
\proofref{In \cref{app:proofs}.}

\begin{remark}[Calibration and tempering compared]
Tempering and calibration both bound over-confidence by a power of $\delta$, but act differently. Tempering
compresses the total $\eta_t$ and leaves the proposal unchanged. Calibration compresses each completion's weight
inside the route sum, so the proposal also looks ahead with $u$ and steers revealed classes towards completions
that are plausible under the flattened law. Errors in the two directions have different costs: by
\eqref{eq:mismatch} a twist that overstates $h_s$ wastes some allocation, whereas one that understates it by
orders of magnitude creates hidden mass. This is the importance-sampling rule that proposals should have heavier
tails than the target, applied to the intermediate targets. Nothing requires $\tau\le1$: for a denoiser whose
predictions are too flat, $\tau>1$ is the corresponding correction.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Look-ahead twists]
\Cref{prop:free} also permits random twists, for instance $\psi_t=\eta_t\hat G_t$ with $\hat G_t$ the potential
product along a speculative rollout, which is an unbiased estimate of $h_t$ when rolled to step $T$. An unbiased
twist does not prevent hidden mass. By \eqref{eq:mismatch} resampling misallocates in proportion to $h_s/\psi_s$,
so what matters is the spread of $\log(h_s/\psi_s)$ across the particles of one run, and rollout noise adds to
that spread. Moreover $\E\log\hat G_t<\log\E\hat G_t$, so when the potential product is heavy-tailed a single
rollout typically falls well below its mean and understates $h_t$, which is the direction that creates hidden
mass. A biased twist that varies smoothly across particles can therefore hide less mass than an unbiased noisy
one. \Cref{sec:expcal} reports the measurement; we do not use look-ahead twists.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Choosing the calibration]
$\tau$, a per-step $\tau_t$, or a richer product law can be fitted from a short pilot run by minimising the
empirical variance of $\log\Zh$. This is controlled SMC \cite{heng2020}, with the family restricted to product
laws so that every route stays exact. A product law cannot represent correlations between future positions, such
as those a copy constraint creates; mixtures of product laws, at one route sum per component, are the next family
that keeps exactness.
\end{remark}

\section{Ambiguity}\label{sec:ambiguity}
%======================================================================

By \cref{thm:traj} an ambiguous route gives the correct target, but it increases variance in two ways: the
proposal favours completions with many derivations, and the twist $\eta_d=\sum_yd(y)q(y)$ overstates how
reachable $C$ is, in proportion to the ambiguity of the remaining completions.

\begin{proposition}[Single-step cost of ambiguity \status{ours}]\label{prop:amb}
Suppose $1\le d(y)\le D$ on $C$. Importance sampling from $q_d\propto d\cdot q\cdot\one_C$ to $q(\cdot\mid C)$ uses
weights $\propto1/d(y)$, with ESS fraction at least $4D/(1+D)^2$, and the twist ratio obeys $1\le\eta_d/\eta\le D$.
\end{proposition}
\proofref{Kantorovich's inequality applied to $X=1/d(y)\in[1/D,1]$; see \cref{app:proofs}.}

The bound is informative when ambiguity is bounded and vacuous when it is not: for the RNA pairing grammar $D$
grows exponentially in $L$. Early in decoding the twist only sees the ambiguity of completions consistent with the
current partial sequence, which can be far smaller than $D$; whether a per-step measure of that kind controls the
ESS of whole trajectories is open (\cref{sec:open}).

In the experiments of \cref{sec:expe1}, resampling increased the error on both ambiguous grammars on every
resampling arm, and calibration did not remove the increase. The chart's twist is derivation-weighted, so
resampling favours partial sequences whose completions have many parse trees over partial sequences with a high
probability of completion. A twist that counts strings rather than derivations would remove this effect, but
exact string counting is \#P-hard for ambiguous grammars in general, so no polynomial-time exact fix is available.
We therefore run ambiguous routes without resampling. Fixed target structures compile to factor graphs, which
count each string once, and are unaffected.

For CFG constraints on hidden Markov models, the exact constrained likelihood is NP-hard already at ambiguity 2,
and an FPRAS exists when ambiguity is polynomially bounded \cite{marzouk2022}. This suggests approximate
string-counting twists under bounded ambiguity, which \cref{prop:free} permits provided the proposal density is
exact and the terminal twist is $\one_C$.

\subsection{Ambiguity}\label{sec:expamb}
Synthetic testbed with grammar routes. At $L=4$ and $L=6$ the sampled law matches the exact conditional to total
variation $\le4\times10^{-16}$ for both ambiguous and unambiguous grammars, as \cref{thm:traj} requires. Ambiguity
affects the weights.
At $K=16$ the ESS fraction over $L=8$ to $32$ holds at $0.93$--$0.97$ for unambiguous Dyck-1 but falls from $0.92$
to $0.34$ for the ambiguous grammar and from $0.89$ to $0.30$ for \textsc{rna-pair}, tracking the mean log
derivation count, which reaches $4.4$ and $5.3$.

\subsection{Is there anything to correct on an mRNA model?}\label{sec:expmrna}
A direct measurement of re-conditioning strength (\cref{sec:meanfield}) before committing to the application.
Holding one codon position masked and revealing the rest of a coding sequence in four stages, the predicted
distribution at the held-out position moves by total variation $0.337$ end to end, or $0.205$ restricted to the
synonymous codons of the true residue, with the largest single movement, $0.291$, in the first quarter of
reveals, which is where step-exact decoding commits. A trajectory mean-field model would give 0 at every stage.
Two related measurements: randomising the whole context moves the prediction by $0.336$, and a synonymous shuffle
preserving the encoded protein still moves it by $0.261$. The model's joint is not a product of per-position
codon tables.

Its \emph{mode}, however, is. On held-out synonymous-codon prediction ($n=548$ paired positions) the model is
top-1 correct 39.8\% of the time against 38.7\% for a human codon-frequency lookup table and 29.5\% for uniform
choice among synonyms; McNemar's test gives $z=+0.53$, not significant. These findings are compatible and say
different things: a product model reproduces the marginal mode and cannot reproduce the joint.

Caveats: one coding sequence, which appears in the model's own configuration and may be in its training data;
the perturbed contexts are somewhat out of distribution; $n=6$ held-out positions for the re-conditioning figures.
These are order-of-magnitude indications and should be repeated across several real human coding sequences.

\section{Application: mRNA sequence design}\label{sec:mrna}
%======================================================================

\subsection{The design problem}
A protein is fixed, since it is the product, but the genetic code is degenerate, so most amino acids have two to
six synonymous codons and a 300-residue protein has on the order of $10^{150}$ coding sequences. They differ in
translation efficiency, stability, folding near the start codon, immunogenicity, and manufacturability: whether a
restriction site breaks the cloning step, whether a homopolymer run causes synthesis errors. Choosing well within
that equivalence class is codon optimisation.

What makes this a good testbed, beyond the application itself, is that mRNA design has something most generation
tasks lack: a deterministic, cheap, non-learned scorer. ViennaRNA computes exact minimum free energy and ensemble
defect. Candidates can be evaluated without trusting a learned reward model, which is the usual weak point of
controllable-generation evaluations.

\subsection{How the system is layered}
Three layers, with strict dependence in one direction. \emph{Layer 1} is everything in
\cref{sec:routes,sec:traj,sec:practice} and knows nothing about biology. \emph{Layer 2} is the codon alphabet,
the genetic code, and constraint builders that return layer-1 route objects over an abstract alphabet.
\emph{Layer 3} is the model adapter.

Protein identity enters \emph{through the route}, as per-position allowed-codon masks, never by renormalising the
model's predictions. This is the methodological point of the whole application: mRNAutilus's own conditional
sampler imposes protein identity by masking logits at each step, which is exactly the step-local projection that
\cref{ex:bias} shows to be biased. Our route imposes the same requirement without touching the trajectory law.

Constraints compile as expected. For a 60-residue protein: motifs alone give a 20-state automaton; motifs plus a
GC band give 2,174 states in 1.0\,s; motifs, GC band and a 7-base-pair hairpin give 968 states, domain 213,
induced width 3, in 0.5\,s. The automaton-times-structure product initially needed $1792^3$ cells and now
enumerates only feasible (codon, state) pairs.

\subsection{What to measure, and what not to}
A survey of the codon-optimisation literature settled a question that changes what we claim. \textbf{No published learned codon-optimisation method reports per-position codon reconstruction accuracy
against a frequency baseline as a primary result.} The single instance in the literature is an unnumbered density
plot at nucleotide resolution in which the frequency baseline is barely separated from uniform random synonymous
choice. The field reports distributional agreement with held-out natural sequences, positional rare-codon
placement, and downstream property prediction instead. Our flat top-1 result in \cref{sec:expmrna} is therefore
not a failure to reproduce published gains; it is a metric those gains were never claimed on. It stays as a
one-line sanity check that the model is not broken, which is what it established.

The constrained families are the headline, because that is where rule-based construction fails outright rather
than merely underperforming: target secondary structure, GC content in sliding windows, motif avoidance where the
forbidden motif straddles a codon boundary, and conjunctions of these. A frequency table cannot satisfy any of
them; a greedy repair heuristic satisfies them while distorting the distribution in ways we can measure exactly.

Planned measurements, in order of how directly they test our claim:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Exactness and coverage.} The fraction of generated sequences satisfying the amino-acid constraint
    exactly (1 by construction for us, measurably less for step-local projection), and the total variation
    between our sampler's law over the synonymous lattice and the model's true conditional, on short coding
    sequences where the lattice can be enumerated. This is the claim no incumbent can state.
  \item \emph{Positional profile against three controls.} CAI, length-normalised MFE, GC and GC3, and a
    positional rare-codon profile distance to the natural sequence, against a frequency-table draw, a max-CAI
    greedy construction, and the natural sequence. The positional term is the discriminating one: it rewards
    reproducing \emph{where} rare codons sit, which a product model is structurally incapable of.
  \item \emph{Best-of-$K$ under a non-learned scorer}, with diversity at fixed $K$ reported alongside, against
    mRNAutilus's own mode-seeking sampler.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why a diverse library, specifically}
Three reasons that stack. Every computational mRNA score correlates imperfectly with what happens in cells, so
taking the argmax under an imperfect score bets everything on its top-ranked point, whereas synthesising many
diverse high-scoring candidates samples the score's upper region. Optimisers with restarts give near-duplicates
around one mode, while a calibrated sampler gives spread at controlled quality. And every candidate must be
manufacturable, so a library where 30\% of members violate a constraint wastes 30\% of the synthesis budget. Hard
guarantees plus diversity is the combination existing methods do not offer together: optimisers give quality
without diversity, unconstrained samplers give diversity without guarantees, and step-local projection gives
guarantees with a silently distorted distribution.

\subsection{State of the implementation}
The model adapter is written and the encoder validated: the vendored transformer block is bit-identical to
upstream's on identical inputs, after fixing a reversed SwiGLU gate order and a missing token-dropout rescaling.
An end-to-end smoke test samples constrained coding sequences, with all particles live, every sequence translating
to the target peptide, and none containing a forbidden restriction site. The design experiments have not been run.

\section{Open directions}\label{sec:open}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Can the twist be made less over-confident without adding noise?}
By \cref{prop:hidden}, over-confidence of the frozen twist causes hidden mass, and by \cref{prop:free} the
intermediate twists can be chosen freely. Calibration (\cref{prop:calibrated}) reduces hidden mass and look-ahead
twists increase it (\cref{sec:expcal}). It is open whether some twist is both smooth across particles and closer
to $h_t$. Candidates are per-step $\tau_t$ fitted from a pilot run, mixtures of product laws for correlated
constraints at one route sum per component, and learned twists $\psi_\vartheta\approx h_t$ fitted to native
rollouts as in \cite{zhao2024}, with $\eta_t$ as a feature.

\subsection{Calibration with resampling off}
With resampling off, calibration at $\tau=0.5$ reduces the relative standard error of $\Zh$ by a factor of $3.3$
on \textsc{counting} and increases it by a factor of $1.17$ on \textsc{copy} (\cref{sec:expcal}). Smoother
per-step increments do not explain the difference. Identifying the mechanism might show which constraints the
calibrated proposal suits.

\subsection{Can hidden mass be bounded online?}
\Cref{prop:hidden} expresses hidden mass through $h_s/\psi_s$, which cannot be observed, and tail diagnostics
fitted to observed $\Zh$ cannot detect it. An estimate or upper bound computable during a run, perhaps from a
small population of particles that is never resampled, would replace the separate resampling-off experiment now
needed to detect it.

\subsection{Does bounded per-step ambiguity control trajectory ESS?}
\Cref{prop:amb} bounds the single-step cost by the global derivation range $D$, which is vacuous when $D$ grows
exponentially. Early in decoding the twist sees only the ambiguity of completions consistent with the current
partial sequence, which can be much smaller. It is open whether a local measure of this kind bounds the ESS of
whole paths.

\subsection{Superset routes}
For a context-free $C$, propose from a regular superset $R\supseteq C$ with twist
$\psi_t=Z_R(x;x)\cdot\mathrm{comp}_C(x)$, where $\mathrm{comp}_C$ tests completability. By \cref{prop:free} this
is exact provided $C\subseteq R$ and $\mathrm{comp}_C$ never rejects a completable state. Reporting 1 for a
dead state only wastes a particle, while a regular \emph{subset} is biased unless the bound provably covers every
string of length $L$. Pruning converts one accept-or-reject at the end into deaths at individual steps, which
resampling can repair, provided $R$ is tight enough to keep the per-step death rate well below $1-1/K$.
A \status{proposed} variant: rather than letting a dead proposal kill its particle, redraw until
$\mathrm{comp}_C$ accepts, and use an unbiased estimate of the single-draw acceptance probability in the
potential, which is valid by the random-twist part of \cref{prop:free}.

\subsection{Where does exact sampling overtake mode-seeking in best-of-$K$?}
Exact conditional sampling should eventually beat mode-seeking decoding as $K$ grows, since the mode is a single
point and the sampler explores the upper region of the score. Where the crossover lies, and how it depends on the
scorer's correlation with the true objective, is open, and is what the mRNA best-of-$K$ experiment
(\cref{sec:mrna}) is designed to measure.

\subsection{Beyond a fixed protein}
The route machinery is indifferent to biology. The natural next targets are better cases for the method than
coding-sequence design: 5$'$UTR design has no protein constraint at all, so the model's joint carries all the
information, and translation initiation depends on structure near the cap and start codon: global constraints
that rules handle badly. Constraints spanning the UTR--CDS boundary are where codon-table methods are
structurally incapable rather than merely worse. Structured text generation is the same algorithm with a
different compile target.

\section{Related work, by component}\label{sec:related}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{The trajectory correction.}
TWISTER \cite{twister} is the direct predecessor: twisted SMC for masked diffusion with DFA constraints,
including the step-exact proposal (\cref{lem:prop}) and the exactness theorem for that case. Zhao et al.\
\cite{zhao2024} apply twisted SMC to autoregressive language models with learned twists. Dang et al.\
\cite{dang2026icml} observe that a derivation-weighted NFA proposal remains valid in autoregressive SMC. The
underlying Feynman--Kac theory, including unbiasedness of $\Zh$ under adaptive resampling, is \cite{delmoral2004}.

\paragraph{Step-local constrained decoding.}
Dang and Ermon \cite{de26} give the automaton-based step-exact decoder that \cref{ex:bias} shows to be biased
across steps; it is our $K=1$ arm. Su and Zhang \cite{sz26} treat constraints as graphs rather than chains, which
corresponds to the factor-graph route here.

\paragraph{What is tractable in one step.}
The circuit characterisation is Broadrick et al.\ \cite{broadrick2025}; the semiring behaviour under ambiguity is
a special case of the compositional atlas of Wang et al.\ \cite{wang2024atlas}. Jerrum, Valiant and Vazirani
\cite{jvv86} give the sampling-to-counting reduction the compiler uses to decide refusals, and Jerrum and Snir
\cite{jerrumsnir1982} the monotone lower bound that motivates the determinant route. Marzouk and de la Higuera
\cite{marzouk2022} give hardness results for CFG-constrained inference in hidden Markov models and an FPRAS under
bounded ambiguity.

\paragraph{The individual routes.}
Grammar charts and top-down sampling are classical; the RNA pairing grammar is Nussinov and Jacobson
\cite{nussinov1980}. The determinant route uses the matrix-tree marginals of Koo et al.\ \cite{koo2007} and
Wilson's sampler \cite{wilson1996}.

\paragraph{The remedies.}
Defensive resampling adapts defensive importance sampling \cite{hesterberg1995} to the ancestor distribution.
Calibrated twists are a restricted, closed-form member of the controlled-SMC family \cite{heng2020}, which fits
twists by regression within a parametric family; restricting to product laws keeps every route exact.

\paragraph{The model.}
Masked diffusion language models follow Sahoo et al.\ \cite{sahoo2024}, whose reverse step defines the
independent schedule of \cref{sec:setup}.

\paragraph{Who else composes constraints of this kind.}
Two axes separate the prior work: which constraint classes a method can express, and which semiring it
evaluates them in. On expressiveness, no decoder matches the set in \cref{tab:routes}: TWISTER
\cite{twister} is trajectory-exact but automaton-only; Su and Zhang \cite{sz26} argue for graphs over
chains and give the factor-graph case; grammar-constrained decoding \cite{geng2023} handles context-free
constraints for autoregressive models by per-step masking, which is biased in the same way
\cref{ex:bias} describes, and grammar-aligned decoding \cite{park2024} removes that bias by iteratively
reweighting across samples rather than within a trajectory.

The closest prior construction is not in the decoding literature at all. LinearDesign \cite{zhang2023}
encodes the synonymous-codon design space of a protein as a weighted DFA and runs \emph{lattice parsing}
against an RNA folding grammar: the automaton-times-grammar conjunction of \cref{app:routes}, built for
exactly our application. The difference is the semiring. LinearDesign evaluates the intersection in
max-times and returns the single optimum of a hand-written objective (free energy traded against codon
adaptation); we evaluate it in sum-product and return samples from a learned model's conditional. By
\cref{prop:semiring} that difference is not cosmetic: the max semiring is correct whatever the grammar's
ambiguity, while the sum semiring counts each string once per derivation. LinearDesign can intersect with
an ambiguous folding grammar and ignore the issue; we cannot, which is what \cref{sec:ambiguity} is about.
So the honest statement of novelty is narrow and worth stating precisely: the conjunction is not new, and
performing it in the sum semiring over a learned distribution is.

\section{Summary}\label{sec:summary}
%======================================================================

\begin{claimbox}{Summary.}
The trajectory-level correction for masked diffusion decoding is independent of the constraint representation:
any route with a matching mass and sampler, plus an exact terminal membership test, gives exact conditional
sampling (\cref{thm:traj}), ambiguous grammars included. If the denoiser's predictions do not change as positions
are revealed, step-exact decoding is already exact (\cref{prop:mf}); the size of this change, the re-conditioning
strength, can be measured before building a sampler. Resampling can leave part of $\E[\Zh]$ in hidden mass
(\cref{prop:hidden}); defensive resampling bounds the added variance independently of the twist, and calibration
reduces hidden mass by an amount that depends on the constraint family. On ambiguous routes resampling increased
the error in our experiments, and the string-counting twist that would avoid this is \#P-hard to compute in
general.
\end{claimbox}

\section{Proofs}\label{app:proofs}
%======================================================================

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:traj}]
(i) By \cref{lem:prop}, $q=\prod_t\kappa_{t+1}Z_d(x_{t+1};x_t)/Z_d(x_t;x_t)$. Multiplying by
\eqref{eq:potential}, the $Z_d$ factors cancel and the twists telescope to
$qW=\psi_0\prod_t\kappa_{t+1}\psi_{t+1}/\psi_t=p\,\psi_T=p\,\one_C$. The divisions are legitimate on the relevant
paths: if $x'$ can still be completed to an element of $C$ then some $y\in C$ agrees with $x'$, so $d(y)>0$ and
hence $Z_d(x';x)>0$ under the positivity assumption, giving $M_{t+1}(x'\mid x)>0$. If $\psi_t(x)=0$ the particle
already has weight zero and we set $0/0=0$.

(ii) follows from (i) by the standard Feynman--Kac argument \cite{delmoral2004}.

(iii) Unbiasedness under multinomial resampling with adaptive decisions is standard \cite{delmoral2004}. For
$\epsilon>0$, let $\mathcal F$ be the particle system just before a resampling step, with weights $W^k$ and mean
$\bar W$. For any $f$,
\[
  \E\Big[\bar W\,\frac1K\sum_j\frac{\wt_{a_j}}{\pi_{a_j}}f(x^{a_j})\Bigm|\mathcal F\Big]
  =\bar W\sum_k\wt_kf(x^k)=\frac1K\sum_kW^kf(x^k),
\]
because $\pi_k>0$ wherever $\wt_k>0$. A resampling step therefore leaves the conditional expectation of the
weighted particle measure unchanged, whether or not it fires. Iterating gives $\E[\Zh]=\E_q[W]=h_0$ by (i).

(iv) At the last step $x_T$ is fully revealed, so $Z_d(x_T;x_{T-1})=d(x_T)$, and \eqref{eq:potential} gives
$G_T=Z_d(x_{T-1};x_{T-1})\one_C(x_T)/\big(\psi_{T-1}(x_{T-1})\,d(x_T)\big)$: the derivation count enters the
denominator and cancels the one the proposal introduced.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{prop:defensive}]
(i) is \cref{thm:traj}(iii). (ii) follows from $\pi_k\ge(1-\epsilon)\wt_k$ and $\pi_k\ge\epsilon/K$. For (iii),
let $Y=(\wt_a/\pi_a)\,g_a$ with $a\sim\pi$. Then $\E Y=\sum_k\wt_kg_k$ and
$\E Y^2=\sum_k\wt_k^2g_k^2/\pi_k\le(K/\epsilon)\sum_k(\wt_kg_k)^2$. The variance of an average of $K$ independent
copies is at most $\E Y^2/K$, and $\sum_k(\wt_kg_k)^2\le(\sum_k\wt_kg_k)^2$ because $g\ge0$. Dividing gives the
bound $1/\epsilon$. Under multinomial resampling the same ratio is
$\frac1K\big(\sum_k\wt_kg_k^2/(\sum_k\wt_kg_k)^2-1\big)$, unbounded when a particle with small $\wt_k$ has large
$g_k$. Taking $g_k=V_s(x^k)$ from \cref{lem:cont} covers the situation of \cref{prop:hidden}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{prop:tempered}]
$\eta_t^\beta>0$ wherever $\eta_t>0$, which includes every completable state, so \cref{prop:free} applies. The
potential is $G_{t+1}=\eta_t(x)^{1-\beta}\eta_{t+1}(x')^\beta/Z_d(x';x)$, and the expression for $c_k/\wt_k$ is
\eqref{eq:mismatch} with $\psi_s=\eta_s^\beta$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{prop:calibrated}]
(i) Fix $r$. $Z^u_d(x';x)$ depends on the tokens revealed on $r$ only through their classes, so summing
$\prod_{i\in r}p^i_\theta(x'_i\mid x)\,Z^u_d(x';x)$ over tokens on $r$ gives
$\sum_c\prod_{i\in r}w_i(c_i\mid x)\,Z^u_d(x'_c;x)=Z^{u,r}_d(x;x)$, where $x'_c$ is $x$ with classes $c$ on $r$.
The marginal on $r$ of the measure $\op{Sample}$ draws from is
$\prod_{i\in r}w_i(c_i\mid x)\,Z^u_d(x'_c;x)/Z^{u,r}_d(x;x)$, and tokens within classes contribute
$p^i_\theta(x'_i\mid x)/w_i(c_i\mid x)$. (ii) is the form of $M^u$. (iii) is \eqref{eq:G} with
$\kappa/M^u=Z^{u,r}_d(x;x)/Z^u_d(x';x)$. (iv) $u_i>0$ wherever $w_i>0$, so $\eta^u_t>0$ wherever $\eta_t>0$,
which includes every state with $h_t>0$, and $M^u>0$ wherever $\kappa h>0$; \cref{prop:free} applies.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{lem:floor}]
Since $\sum_\rho w_i(\rho)=1$ and $0<\tau\le1$, Hölder gives $1\le\sum_\rho w_i(\rho)^\tau\le|\Sigma|^{1-\tau}$,
so $|\Sigma|^{-(1-\tau)}w_i^\tau\le u_i\le w_i^\tau$ at every masked position. Multiply over positions and sum
against $d$. For the lower bound use $(\sum_jb_j)^\tau\le\sum_jb_j^\tau$, counting each derivation of $y$ as one
term. The last claim uses $h_t\le1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{prop:amb}]
Let $X=1/d(y)$ with $y\sim q_d$, so $X\in[m,M]$ with $m=1/D$, $M=1$, and write $\mu=\E X$. From
$(X-m)(M-X)\ge0$ we get $\E X^2\le(m+M)\mu-mM$. The quadratic $\frac{(m+M)^2}{4mM}\mu^2-(m+M)\mu+mM$ has zero
discriminant, hence is non-negative, giving $\E X^2\le\frac{(m+M)^2}{4mM}\mu^2$ (Kantorovich). Substituting
$m=1/D$, $M=1$ gives the ESS bound; the twist bound is immediate from $1\le d\le D$.
\end{proof}

\section{Route implementations}\label{app:routes}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{Automata.}
For $(Q,q_0,\delta,F)$ over $\Sigma$, the forward recursion
$\alpha_0(q)=[q=q_0]$, $\alpha_i(q)=\sum_{q',\sigma:q\in\delta(q',\sigma)}\alpha_{i-1}(q')w_{i,\sigma}$,
$\op{Mass}=\sum_{q\in F}\alpha_L(q)$ costs $O(L|\delta|)$. $\op{Sample}$ is forward filtering, backward sampling:
draw $q_L\propto\alpha_L(q)[q\in F]$, then for $i=L,\dots,1$ draw $(q_{i-1},\sigma_i)$ with probability
$\propto\alpha_{i-1}(q_{i-1})w_{i,\sigma_i}[q_i\in\delta(q_{i-1},\sigma_i)]$. For a DFA $d=\one_C$; for an NFA
$d(y)$ counts accepting paths. Compiles: forbidden and required motifs via Aho--Corasick, counting conditions
($O(Lk)$ states), regular expressions over classes, bounded bracket depth, sliding-window all-different, and
products of these. The forward pass must be in float64. In float32 it returned $Z=0$ for a feasible constraint at
log-mass $-113.5$.

\paragraph{Grammar charts.}
For a grammar in Chomsky normal form, the inside algorithm
\[
  \beta(A,i,i{+}1)=\sum_\sigma[A\to\sigma]w_{i,\sigma},\qquad
  \beta(A,i,j)=\sum_{A\to BC}\sum_{i<k<j}\beta(B,i,k)\beta(C,k,j),\qquad
  \op{Mass}=\beta(S,0,L)
\]
costs $O(L^3|G|)$; leaves for all positions come from one matrix product of the denoiser output with the lexical
map. $\op{Sample}$ pushes $(S,0,L)$ and repeatedly pops $(A,i,j)$: at a leaf draw $\sigma\propto[A\to\sigma]
w_{i,\sigma}$, otherwise draw $(A\to BC,k)\propto\beta(B,i,k)\beta(C,k,j)$ and push both children. This draws each
parse tree with probability proportional to its weight, so the sampled string has probability
$\propto d(y)\prod_iw_{i,y_i}$. On a GPU all spans of one length are processed together. Per-nonterminal span
limits (a subtree rooted at a listed nonterminal covers at most $W$ positions) cut the work to $O(L^2W|G|)$ when
every nonterminal outside a right-branching spine is bounded.

\paragraph{Factor graphs.}
With $0/1$ factors and an elimination order of induced width $\omega$, variable elimination gives $\op{Mass}$ in
$O(L|\Sigma|^{\omega+1})$, and $\op{Sample}$ eliminates in that order and samples in reverse, each bucket table
being the exact conditional. Derivations are assignments, so $d=\one_C$, and fixed target structures are
unaffected by ambiguity. The motivating case is the same-order copy relation \cite{sz26}, of width 1:
$\op{Mass}=\prod_{i\le k}\sum_\sigma w_{i,\sigma}w_{i+k,\sigma}\times\prod_{i>2k}\sum_\sigma w_{i,\sigma}$.
Elimination cost is governed by the induced width of the order, and the smallest width over all orders is
the treewidth; finding an optimal order is NP-hard \cite{arnborg1987}, so we use the greedy minimum-degree
heuristic (George and Liu, SIAM Review 31(1):1--19, 1989).

\paragraph{Determinants.}
Let $w_m(h)$ be the weight that node $m$ has head $h$, and $\hat L$ the reduced Laplacian with
$\hat L_{mm}=\sum_{h\ne m}w_m(h)$ and $\hat L_{hm}=-w_m(h)$. The directed matrix-tree theorem gives
$\op{Mass}=\det\hat L$, and differentiating $\log\det\hat L$ gives the marginals
$\mu_m(h)=w_m(h)([\hat L^{-1}]_{mm}-[h\ne0][\hat L^{-1}]_{mh})$ \cite{koo2007}; both cost $O(n^3)$. $\op{Sample}$
is Wilson's algorithm \cite{wilson1996}: for each node not yet in the tree, run the random walk moving $m\to h$
with probability $w_m(h)/\sum_{h'}w_m(h')$ until it meets the tree, erase loops, and add the path.

\paragraph{From classes to tokens.}
A route draws a class sequence $y$; each revealed position then draws a token within its class,
$v\propto p^i_\theta(v\mid x)[\phi(v)=y_i]$. The combined token-level law is
$\prod_ip^i_\theta(v_i\mid x)\one_C(\phi(v))/Z$, which is what the trajectory layer requires. Class masses must be
computed by exact per-class log-sum-exp rather than a floored sum: flooring at $10^{-38}$ inflates rare classes
and biases the weights in the rare-event regime.

\paragraph{Conjunctions.}
Automaton with automaton gives a product automaton. Automaton with grammar gives a grammar, by the Bar-Hillel
triple construction, of size at most $|R|Q^3$ and usually far less. Automaton with factor graph gives a factor
graph over augmented variables $z_i=(y_i,s_i)$, with the automaton contributing a chain of pairwise factors;
restricting to feasible (symbol, state) pairs per position is what makes this practical. Two grammars, or a
grammar with a factor graph, are refused, as is any conjunction whose counting problem is \#P-hard.

\section{Experimental protocol}\label{app:protocol}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{Estimators and diagnostics.}
$\Zh$ ratios are reported against $h_0$ estimated from a large native sample, with bootstrap confidence intervals
that include the binomial uncertainty in $h_0$. $\hat k$ is the generalised-Pareto shape of the upper weight tail,
fitted as in Pareto-smoothed importance sampling with the Zhang--Stephens estimator; $\hat k>0.5$ indicates
infinite variance, $>0.7$ that the plain mean is unreliable, $>1$ infinite mean. Sample quality is pairwise total
variation between empirical joint class distributions of every position pair, averaged over pairs, measured
against a rejection reference and reported in excess of a matched sampling floor.

\paragraph{Correctness checks.}
Each route is verified against brute-force enumeration at small $L$; the sampler is verified for $\E[\Zh]=h_0$;
golden regression values pin the RNG stream so that refactors cannot silently change results. These checks found
four bugs, none of which caused a crash: a float32 automaton forward pass returning
$Z=0$ at log-mass $-113.5$; class probabilities floored at $10^{-38}$ biasing weights in the rare-event regime;
padded columns counted as reveals under the independent schedule, biasing $\Zh$ by about 30\%; and a scheduler
refactor that moved the reveal draw ahead of the route sample, changing the RNG order.

\paragraph{Reproduction.}
Result files and artifact identifiers are listed in \texttt{docs/status.md}. The test suite covers compilation,
CPU and batched samplers, SMC, resampling, diagnostics, exact laws, alphabets, and the biology layers.
